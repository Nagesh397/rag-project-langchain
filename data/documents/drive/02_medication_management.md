## 1. Purpose, Scope, and Responsibilities

This policy describes safe medication ordering, verification, storage, preparation, administration, monitoring, reconciliation, and disposal. It applies to licensed prescribers, pharmacists, nurses, technicians, and other staff authorized by credentialing and scope of practice. Medication use must follow the approved formulary, patient-specific order, professional standards, and applicable law.

The prescriber is responsible for a complete and clinically appropriate order. Pharmacy verifies orders, resolves discrepancies, maintains medication information, and supports safe preparation. Nursing verifies the patient, medication, dose, route, time, indication, and relevant parameters before administration. Each professional must stop and clarify an order that appears incomplete, unsafe, or inconsistent with the patient condition.

Medication administration is not a task to be completed by an unauthorized person. Students and observers may participate only under approved supervision. Verbal or telephone orders are limited to permitted situations and must be read back and documented.

## 2. Ordering, Verification, and Reconciliation

Medication orders must use the approved electronic or paper format and include the medication name, dose, route, frequency, indication when required, start and stop information, and necessary parameters. Avoid prohibited abbreviations and trailing zeros. The medication list should be reviewed when a patient is admitted, transferred, and discharged.

Medication reconciliation compares the patient’s prior medication use with new orders. Staff should ask the patient or reliable source about prescription medicines, non-prescription products, vitamins, supplements, allergies, and prior reactions. Differences must be resolved, not merely copied into a list. The responsible clinician decides which medications continue, change, or stop.

Before dispensing or administration, staff must check allergies, interactions, duplicate therapy, contraindications, renal or hepatic considerations, weight-based dosing requirements, and relevant laboratory or vital-sign parameters. A patient’s identity must be verified using two approved identifiers; room number is not an identifier.

## 3. Storage, Preparation, and High-Alert Medicines

Medications are stored in clean, secure, labeled areas with temperature and access controls appropriate to the product. Expired, damaged, recalled, or unidentified medicines are removed from active stock and handled through pharmacy instructions. Refrigerated medicines require documented temperature monitoring and excursion response.

High-alert medicines are those with a heightened risk of significant harm when used incorrectly. The hospital maintains a current high-alert list. Examples may include concentrated electrolytes, insulin, anticoagulants, opioids, sedatives, and chemotherapy agents. Required safeguards may include standardized concentrations, restricted storage, barcode verification, independent double checks, smart-pump libraries, and monitoring protocols.

Preparation must occur in a designated clean area with interruption controls. Labels must identify the patient, medication, concentration, preparation time, expiry or beyond-use time, and preparer as required. Never administer a syringe or container that is unlabeled or cannot be reliably identified.

## 4. Administration and Monitoring

At the bedside, the administering professional confirms the patient using two identifiers and compares the medication with the order and label. The professional confirms the medication, dose, route, time, indication, allergies, relevant assessments, and required patient education. Barcode scanning is a safety control and must not be bypassed without documenting the reason and following downtime procedures.

Patients should be told the medication name or purpose, important expected effects, and symptoms that require immediate reporting when appropriate. Refusals must be respected, documented, and communicated to the responsible clinician when the medication is clinically important.

After administration, staff monitor the intended effect and possible adverse reaction according to medication and patient risk. Suspected severe reactions require immediate clinical response and notification. Medication errors and near misses must be reported through the patient safety process even when the patient is unharmed.

## 5. Controlled Substances, Disposal, and Audit

Controlled substances are stored in secured locations with access limited to authorized staff. Removal, administration, waste, return, and discrepancy processes must produce an auditable record. Waste must be witnessed when required. A discrepancy is escalated immediately to the supervisor and pharmacy; staff must not attempt to correct records informally.

Unused medications are returned or discarded using approved pharmaceutical and environmental procedures. Sharps and contaminated materials follow the hospital waste policy. Patients and families must not take hospital medication home unless discharge procedures authorize it and the medication is properly reconciled and labeled.

Pharmacy and nursing leaders review medication incidents, override patterns, refrigerator records, controlled-substance discrepancies, and compliance with independent checks. Corrective actions are tracked to completion. This policy must be reviewed after significant regulatory change, formulary change, or a serious medication event.
