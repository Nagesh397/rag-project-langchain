# Patient Safety and Incident Reporting Policy

**Owner:** Quality and Patient Safety Department  
**Classification:** Internal hospital policy  
**Effective date:** 2026-01-01

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This policy establishes a consistent process for recognizing, reporting, reviewing, and learning from events that may harm a patient, visitor, staff member, or the hospital. It applies to all employees, contractors, students, volunteers, credentialed clinicians, and departments operating under the hospital license. Reporting is intended to improve systems and reduce repeat risk. It is not a substitute for immediate clinical treatment, emergency escalation, or a formal employment investigation when those are required.

A safety event includes an actual injury, an adverse clinical outcome, a medication discrepancy, a near miss, an unsafe condition, a device or equipment problem, a privacy concern, a fall, a specimen problem, or a delay that could affect care. Staff should report concerns even when the outcome was prevented or no harm is visible. A near miss is valuable information because it shows where controls almost failed.

The policy applies to inpatient, outpatient, emergency, diagnostic, pharmacy, transport, environmental, and administrative settings. Department leaders must make this policy available during orientation and annual safety training.

## 2. Immediate Response

The first responsibility is to protect the patient and stabilize the situation. Staff must call for clinical help according to the department emergency process, provide appropriate first aid or treatment within their competence, and notify the responsible clinician. Emergency services must be activated for a life-threatening event. Do not delay care in order to complete a report.

After immediate care, preserve relevant evidence. Do not discard medication packaging, device components, labels, specimens, or records that may be needed for review. Secure the area when there is a continuing hazard. Record objective facts in the clinical record when the event concerns patient care; do not place blame, speculation, or internal review commentary in the clinical record.

The patient and family should receive timely, honest communication from the responsible clinician and leader. Disclosure must follow the hospital disclosure process. Staff should not promise a particular outcome, assign fault, or discuss confidential personnel information. The quality team may support communication, documentation, and follow-up.

## 3. Reporting Process

The person who discovers or experiences the event should notify the immediate supervisor as soon as practical and submit an electronic safety report before the end of the shift, or as soon as the person is able. If the event is serious, the supervisor must notify the administrator on call and the quality and patient safety department immediately.

A report should state what happened, when and where it happened, who was involved by approved identifier, what was observed, what immediate actions were taken, and what equipment or records may be relevant. Use neutral language. Write “the medication was found on the floor” rather than “the nurse carelessly dropped the medication.” Do not copy the entire clinical record into the incident report.

Reports are confidential quality-improvement records to the extent allowed by applicable law and policy. Access is limited to people with a legitimate review responsibility. Staff may report anonymously when the reporting system permits, but contact information helps investigators clarify facts and provide feedback.

## 4. Review and Corrective Action

The quality department classifies the event by actual and potential severity. Serious events receive immediate executive review and a structured investigation. Lower-severity events may be grouped for trend analysis. Reviews examine processes, communication, workload, environment, training, technology, equipment, and policy design. The purpose is to identify contributing conditions, not to assume that the last person involved caused the event.

Corrective actions must have an owner, due date, success measure, and review date. Examples include changing a form, separating look-alike medications, adding an independent verification step, repairing equipment, revising staffing escalation, or improving handoff information. Education alone should not be the only action when a system redesign is reasonably available.

Department leaders are responsible for implementing approved actions and providing evidence of completion. The quality department monitors overdue actions and reports trends through the patient safety committee. Repeated events require reassessment even when individual corrective actions were marked complete.

## 5. Learning, Non-Retaliation, and Records

The hospital shares de-identified lessons through safety huddles, staff meetings, newsletters, alerts, and training. Shared learning must protect patient privacy and avoid identifying individual staff unless disclosure is authorized and necessary. Staff are encouraged to raise concerns early. Retaliation against a person who reports a good-faith safety concern, participates in a review, or asks for help is prohibited.

Safety reports are retained according to the hospital records schedule and applicable legal requirements. The quality department controls access, tracks review status, and documents action closure. Requests for records from outside the review process must be referred to the privacy or legal function.

This policy does not replace emergency response, mandatory reporting, infection-control notification, safeguarding procedures, or regulatory reporting. When more than one process applies, staff should begin the most urgent response and notify the supervisor. Questions should be directed to the Quality and Patient Safety Department.
